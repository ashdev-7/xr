# Speed information unlocks PathGuard's lost trajectory amplitude

A full-text review of inertial and visual-inertial odometry since 2017, combined with PathGuard's own ablations, leads to one conclusion. Almost all of PathGuard's remaining error is missing low-frequency amplitude. The one lever that both the literature and PathGuard's measurements support is a **non-zero-mean speed observation**. Attitude, drag-model refinements, bias states, robust filtering and blind-window rules are not that lever. PathGuard's frequency-resolved transfer gain falls from 0.8–1.0 at 2–20 s periods to **0.1–0.4 above 60 s**. That is the high-pass behaviour that Gaussian-process smoothing theory predicts for any fixed zero-mean motion prior paired with weak long-period information (project measurement; mechanism from [Anderson et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.0630) and [Hartikainen & Särkkä](https://users.aalto.fi/~ssarkka/pub/gp-ts-kfrts.pdf)). PathGuard's speed oracle shows the ceiling is large. Feeding the true 1 s speed as a measurement cut TRAIN ATE by **17–28% on four drone flights and 6–36% on two handheld rooms** (project measurement). That beats every other ceiling PathGuard has measured: perfect attitude −0.8%/−5%, rest updates 1.6%, spike repair 0%, blind rule ≤1%. The literature's template is Solin's speed-norm pseudo-measurement and its learned successor ([Solin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.00154); [Cortes et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03485)). Neither has ever been tested on a multirotor, and every published learned-velocity network is 10–10⁴× too heavy for a pure-Python 20 ms/s budget. The ranked plan therefore runs as follows:
1. Attribute the high-pass to a specific filter component.
2. Find out how much speed error the oracle gain survives.
3. Test a constant per-platform speed norm.
4. Then build a stdlib ridge speed regressor trained on degraded logs.

A mean-reverting velocity prior, a per-flight drag-coefficient ceiling and a thrust-axis audit are secondary bets.

## The amplitude loss is a high-pass filter the theory predicts

PathGuard's frequency-resolved measurement settles where the error lives (project measurement):

| Period band | Transfer gain |
|---|---|
| 2–20 s | 0.8–1.0 |
| 20–60 s | 0.5–0.8 |
| Above 60 s | 0.1–0.4 |

On MH_02 the gain is **0.12 on the band that holds 79% of the motion**. The continuous-time literature explains why a smoother built from zero-mean motion pseudo-measurements must behave this way. White noise passed through a linear SDE yields a coloured-noise GP prior. A Kalman/RTS smoother on that state-space model returns exactly the GP-regression posterior, so PathGuard's smoother is a fixed linear Wiener filter ([Anderson et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.0630); [Hartikainen & Särkkä](https://users.aalto.fi/~ssarkka/pub/gp-ts-kfrts.pdf)).

Two further results are derivations by the GP-notes author, not statements from a paper. First, a "v = 0" pseudo-measurement is a Wiener prior on position, and adding "p − c = 0" makes it an Ornstein–Uhlenbeck prior. Second, the accelerometer error spectrum includes a bias random walk that maps to ω⁻⁶ in position. So with IMU alone the posterior gain on long-period motion tends to zero like ω², and with drag velocity it tends to a constant H₀ = 1/(1 + R_d/S_v + R_d/σ_c²) below one.

PathGuard's measured gain keeps falling above 60 s instead of levelling off. That is an inference with a direct implication: on the drones, drag contributes little usable long-period information, and the drones behave almost like the IMU-only case. The VIO notes reach a parallel hypothesis from another direction. A world-frame random-walk accelerometer bias can absorb real low-frequency acceleration and so act as a high-pass on position. ORB-SLAM3's designers took the opposite approach and constrained the bias: a zero-mean prior on b_a that starts near-pinned and is relaxed only after 5 s and 15 s ([ORB-SLAM3 code](https://github.com/UZ-SLAMLab/ORB_SLAM3/blob/master/src/LocalMapping.cc)). Campos et al. observe that before visual bundle adjustment "all methods tend to underestimate the true scale" ([Campos et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2003.05766)). No experiment has yet separated the motion prior from the bias random walk as the dominant cause. That separation is the first item in the plan.

The literature also explains why "just upscale" fails. Under SE(3)-aligned RMSE, scaling a centred estimate by c gives N·RMSE²(c) = c²X − 2cσ + P. This is minimised at the Umeyama Sim(3) scale, and the optimal amplitude ratio κ* equals the shape correlation ρ (derivation in the evaluation notes, built on evo's position-only Umeyama alignment, [evo trajectory.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MichaelGrupp/evo/master/evo/core/trajectory.py)). Shrinkage is therefore optimal whenever the long-period shape is poorly known. The amplitude problem is really an information problem: the cure has to raise ρ in the >60 s band, not just κ. Every prior-reshaping idea in the literature is a fixed filter that trades one environment against another. This includes WNOJ, Singer, Matérn sums and rational-quadratic scale mixtures ([Tang et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1809.06518); [Wong et al.](https://www.dynsyslab.org/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/wong-ral20.pdf); [Särkkä & Solin](https://users.aalto.fi/~asolin/sde-book/sde-book.pdf)). Every published method that learns prior hyperparameters (Anderson, Wong) needs ground truth. Wong lists learning without full ground truth as future work ([Wong et al.](https://www.dynsyslab.org/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/wong-ral20.pdf)). PathGuard's rejection of a Student-t scale-mixture workspace prior (TRAIN ATE 2.488 → 2.540–2.827, project measurement) fits this picture. Reweighting a prior adds no information.

One prior change remains untested and is principled: an OU (Singer-type) velocity prior that separates "how fast" (σ_v) from "how persistent" (τ_v). Wong et al. found a Singer prior beat white-noise-on-acceleration by 5.64% in translation and stayed robust through a 4 s dropout ([Wong et al.](https://www.dynsyslab.org/wp-content/papercite-data/pdf/wong-ral20.pdf)). In PathGuard's setting it raises low-frequency prior power without loosening fast motion (inference). It cannot add information, though. Its ceiling is bounded by what the drag and IMU signals already contain at long periods.

## Drone physics is close to exhausted

The rotor-drag literature agrees on one point: IMU-only drag aiding bounds body-frame horizontal velocity, roll and pitch, and nothing else. The horizontal accelerometers on a multirotor measure drag, not gravity tilt ([Abeywardena et al. 2015](https://arxiv.org/abs/1509.03388); [Martin & Sarras](https://arxiv.org/abs/1510.03249)). Vertical velocity needs rotor speed plus ESC power ([Allibert et al.](https://opus.lib.uts.edu.au/bitstream/10453/32303/4/Estimating%20body-fixed%20frame%20velocity%20and%20attitude%20from%20inertial%20measurements%20for%20a%20quadrotor%20vehicle.pdf)). The closest published system to PathGuard is Xu & de Croon. It runs on the same EuRoC Firefly, carries k_d as an EKF state initialised at 0.2, and couples the bias into the drag measurement. It still reports that the drag-aided horizontal states "are affected by the accelerometer bias". Even with visual yaw and translation-direction updates, its machine-hall ATE is **1.56–13.2 m** (table units not printed, presumably metres) ([Xu & de Croon](https://repository.tudelft.nl/record/uuid:c07670f7-7ee4-4a18-a0f0-d3366d99ac8f)). Abeywardena 2016 found the inertial-only velocity biased until vision was added, which made the accelerometer bias estimable ([Abeywardena et al. 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1607.01486)). Every method that separates bias, drag and external force uses rotor speeds or thrust: VIMO, VID-Fusion, HDVIO, NeuroBEM, and Svacha 2019, which is abstract-only here. VIMO's authors state they could not use EuRoC because it lacks rotor speeds ([VIMO](https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss15/p82.pdf); [HDVIO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.11429)).

PathGuard's measurements line up with this literature point for point. Coupling the bias into drag made TRAIN ATE much worse (2.488 → 3.534). A body-frame bias oracle is worth only −0.5%. Anisotropic, thrust-scaled and quadratic drag gave +2.4%, −0.2% and no signal (all project measurements). The literature predicts these nulls. NeuroBEM and Faessler treat linear drag as adequate at low speed ([NeuroBEM](https://arxiv.org/abs/2106.08015); [Faessler et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1712.02402)). VIMO and HDVIO show that constant model offsets are absorbed as bias. The notes' derivation gives a constant body bias b an unobservable direction: tilt error b/g and velocity error b/μ, broken only by varying yaw rate (inference).

The perfect-attitude ceiling of **−0.8% on drones** (project measurement) is decisive for two drone-notes proposals. Both assume tilt errors cause the amplitude loss: the accelerometer-aided attitude shrinkage test and the full-state tilt-and-velocity drag filter. If perfect tilt is worth 0.8%, neither can recover a 2× shrink (inference).

The per-flight ground-truth drag fit (μ of **0.09–0.22, no consistent hall-versus-room difference**, project measurement) overturns the earlier report's thesis. That thesis held that a single μ under-reads one environment and over-reads the other. The per-flight spread is still a factor of about 2.4, and drag velocity scales as 1/μ. So a per-flight μ oracle is worth one cheap run before the drag line is closed. Xu & de Croon carry μ as a state, but they never report its accuracy against ground truth. Leishman's IMU-only μ estimation is known only from its abstract ([BYU record](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/1419)). One audit is also untested. If the thrust axis is defined as the mean airborne specific force, the body-frame mean of f_perp is zero by construction. That would erase μ times any true mean forward velocity, which is a candidate source of the drag residual's mean offset (inference, drone notes). The lever-arm term ω × r in Burri's rotor-drag model is likewise unmodelled ([arXiv 1612.05143](https://arxiv.org/abs/1612.05143)).

## Speed observations are the only lever with literature and a measured ceiling

Every learned inertial odometry method gets metric amplitude from supervised regression of velocity or displacement over 1–2 s windows. No method reads it off double integration ([RIDI](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.09004); [RoNIN](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12853); [TLIO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01867)). Among classical systems, only Solin's handheld INS adds a **non-zero-mean** pseudo-measurement, ‖v‖ = 0.75 m/s with noise N(0, 2²), applied when not stationary ([Solin et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.00154)). Its annular likelihood penalises "too slow" as much as "too fast", so it does not shrink. Cortes et al. replaced the constant with a CNN regression of 2 s momentary speed. Test RMSE was 0.20 m/s, and fused RMSE fell from **238.38 m with no speed constraint to 0.87 m with the constant, and 0.62 m with the learned speed**. Position fixes arrived every 17 s ([Cortes et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1808.03485)). On drones, AirIO regresses body-frame velocity and averages **3.177 m** EuRoC ATE with an EKF. The setup is clean data with unstated alignment and ground-truth attitude in training. Its body-frame inputs beat global-frame inputs (4.730 vs 10.096) ([AirIO](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15659)).

PathGuard's speed oracle quantifies the prize under PathGuard's own fusion (project measurements; percentages are this writer's arithmetic):

| Flight | Baseline TRAIN ATE (m) | With oracle speed (m) | Change |
|---|---|---|---|
| MH_01 | 3.46 | 2.59 | −25% |
| MH_03 | 1.93 | 1.39 | −28% |
| V1_01 | 1.37 | 1.12 | −18% |
| V2_02 | 1.38 | 1.15 | −17% |
| room1 | 0.317 | 0.297 | −6% |
| room3 | 0.510 | 0.324 | −36% |

The oracle fed the true 1 s speed as a horizontal velocity measurement along the pass-1 velocity direction, with σ = 0.3 m/s.

These gains match or exceed the −18% per-flight prior-size oracle on three of four drone flights. They are largest on the machine hall, where the high-pass bites hardest. The oracle needs no direction information beyond PathGuard's own first pass. So the missing quantity is speed magnitude, not heading, which is consistent with the −0.8% attitude ceiling (inference).

Three findings from the literature temper the expectation. First, even networks trained on 20+ hours carry **6–20% path-length error** (RIO's D-drift metric; [RIO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.11676)). Second, no paper evaluates a speed pseudo-measurement on a multirotor. RNIN-VIO lists "drone movement" among motions its pedestrian network fails on ([RNIN-VIO](https://zju3dv.github.io/rnin-vio.github.io/files/ismar21b-sub2280-cam-i5.pdf)). Third, capacity matters: shrinking AirIO's latent to 64 raised its body-frame EuRoC ATE by 45.4% ([AirIO](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15659)). With TRAIN median horizontal speeds of **0.48 m/s on drones and 0.83 m/s handheld** (project measurement), Cortes's 0.20 m/s RMSE would be about 42% of drone speed and 24% of handheld speed (arithmetic). The oracle must therefore be re-run with realistic corruption before anyone builds a regressor.

What fits the budget is a structural copy, not a network. PathGuard measures 6–7 ms per second of IMU and 1.7–1.9 MB of heap, which leaves about 13 ms/s and 6 MB of headroom (project measurement and arithmetic). A ridge regressor on 20–40 windowed features at 10 Hz needs roughly 300–1,200 multiply-adds per second plus about 6,000 operations of running-sum feature extraction. The notes estimate 0.5–2 ms/s; this is an estimate, not a measurement. The literature gives the design rules:

| Design rule | Source |
|---|---|
| Use yaw-invariant features: vertical components, horizontal norms, horizontal dot products | [EqNIO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2408.06321), [RoNIN](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.12853) |
| Train on bias- and tilt-perturbed inputs, which here means the official degradation script with several seeds | [TLIO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01867) |
| Inflate the measurement variance about 10× for overlapping windows, and chi-square gate | [TLIO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.01867), [RNIN-VIO](https://zju3dv.github.io/rnin-vio.github.io/files/ismar21b-sub2280-cam-i5.pdf) |
| Switch the measurement off below about 0.5 m/s, where direction is ambiguous | [RIO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2111.11676) |
| Use mode-specific regressors; an all-in-one SVR was about 20× worse in MSE | [RIDI](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.09004) |
| Add cumulative-displacement rows that penalise long-horizon speed bias; this loss cut RNIN's ATE from 9.65 to 3.38 on one split (reconstructed table) | [RNIN-VIO](https://zju3dv.github.io/rnin-vio.github.io/files/ismar21b-sub2280-cam-i5.pdf) |

IDOL's cross-building failure and IMO's failure on unseen track shapes both mean the regressor must be validated leave-one-scene-out, not only leave-one-flight-out ([IDOL](https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.04024); [IMO](https://arxiv.org/html/2210.15287v2)).

## Robustness, blind-window and evaluation work is already capped

The robust-estimation literature mostly reports parity on clean data. Roth et al. state that "simulations without maneuvers or outliers yield similar performance for all algorithms". They also warn that gating "often leads to divergence" because manoeuvres and outliers both produce large residuals ([Roth et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1703.02428)). Variational adaptive filters recover the gap between a wrong and a true covariance: 4.63 → 2.81 m against 2.77 m with the true covariance in Huang's simulation ([Huang et al.](https://figshare.le.ac.uk/articles/journal_contribution/A_Novel_Adaptive_Kalman_Filter_with_Inaccurate_Process_and_Measurement_Noise_Covariance_Matrices/10197092/1)). They adapt on measurement innovations, and PathGuard has none outside rest periods because the IMU is its propagation input (inference). The large zero-velocity gains are all foot-mounted: over 50% from regime-switched thresholds and 32.6% from an LSTM detector ([Wahlström & Skog](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.09208); [Wagstaff et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1910.00529)). Those IMUs stop on every step, which EuRoC and TUM-VI do not.

PathGuard's ceilings bound all of this: spike repair 0%, rest updates 1.6%, and a blind-window forecast rule worth ≤1%. Masked backtests show blind error is set by the position error at failure (project measurements). The forecasting literature's decaying-velocity rule is therefore not a lever. Neither are soft ZUPT, VB-AKF or Student-t process noise. The blind window improves only when the whole trajectory improves (inference).

On evaluation, the literature offers no like-for-like comparator. AirIO's 2.3–4.2 m per test flight is the only full-trajectory IMU-only EuRoC ATE found. It uses clean data, unstated alignment and different test flights ([AirIO](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15659)). TinyOdom's 2.19–2.90 m is 2D, on unnamed files ([TinyOdom](https://par.nsf.gov/servlets/purl/10385251)). No paper reports a stand-still baseline, and none reports IMU-only ATE on TUM-VI. Against its own stand-still baselines PathGuard scores **2.37 m vs 3.20 m on drones (ratio 0.74) and 0.53 m vs 1.10 m handheld (ratio 0.48)** (project measurement; ratios are arithmetic). These ratios, plus the per-flight Sim(3) scale and band gains, are the honest way to report progress. The evaluation notes add one protocol detail: evo matches nearest stamps within 0.01 s without de-duplication, so rows with duplicate timestamps should carry identical poses ([evo sync.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/MichaelGrupp/evo/master/evo/core/sync.py)).

## Conclusion

The review changes the diagnosis. PathGuard no longer has a drag-model problem, a tilt problem or a robustness problem. It has a **long-period information problem**, and it now has the measurements to prove it. Band gains show where the motion is lost. Ceilings show that attitude, bias, rest and blind-window work cannot recover it. The speed oracle shows that a scalar non-zero-mean observation does. The literature confirms the mechanism: only supervised speed or velocity regression, or Solin's constant speed norm, injects amplitude without a camera. But it offers no evidence that this works on a multirotor, and nothing small enough to copy directly. The decisive unknowns are empirical and cheap to resolve. They are how much speed error the oracle gain tolerates (rank 2), and whether degraded hexacopter vibration and drag signals carry speed at that accuracy (rank 4).

If rank 2 shows the gain collapses below about 0.1 m/s speed error, PathGuard is near the information limit of IMU-only odometry on these logs. Its stand-still ratios of 0.74 (drone) and 0.48 (handheld) would then be the result to defend. That is a defensible position: no published IMU-only method reports a stand-still baseline at all, and none has run on degraded data inside a 20 ms/s pure-Python budget.
