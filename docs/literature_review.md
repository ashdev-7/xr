# PathGuard's physics beats learned odometry where it counts

PathGuard's architecture is sound and close to what the literature recommends for an offline, IMU-only, compute-starved estimator. Its world-frame error-state attitude filter is the formulation with the least state-dependent Jacobians. Its RTS smoothers are equivalent to batch MAP for the linear parts. Its rotor-drag velocity measurement is the established Abeywardena/Leishman model, and its held-out EuRoC numbers are in the same band as the best published learned inertial odometry (AirIO-EKF averages 3.18 m on clean EuRoC), even though PathGuard runs on degraded data with a 20 ms/s pure-Python budget. On "scale", the injected sensor scale-factor drift is correctly treated as negligible. The real scale problem is trajectory amplitude: PathGuard shrinks machine-hall flights by about 2x and slightly overshoots Vicon-room flights. The literature points to a likely mechanism the team has not tested. In a drag-aided IMU-only system, velocity is only known up to the drag coefficient mu, so amplitude and mu are confounded much as scale is in monocular vision. The fact that the drag marginal likelihood rises monotonically with prior size is the signature of correlated drag errors being treated as white noise, not proof that amplitude is unidentifiable. The highest-value missing work is therefore on the drag model and its noise model (thrust-scaled and anisotropic mu, a properly coloured or decimated measurement, then a re-test of likelihood-based prior sizing), followed by robust reweighting of residual spikes, s²-consistent noise scaling with a few EM iterations, and noise-adaptive rest detection. Teams with learned models can beat PathGuard on machine-hall flights only if they can run a network inside the budget and train it on degraded data. No published learned method does either.

## The estimator matches the textbook choices at every layer

The attitude filter uses a global (world-frame) angular error. In Solà's formulation that makes the error dynamics δθ̇ = −R·δω_b − R·ω_n, independent of the angular rate, and the rotation block of the transition matrix becomes the identity ([Solà 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.02508)). With a world-frame gravity measurement, the attitude Jacobian is [g_w]× with no dependence on the estimate, so yaw sits exactly in the measurement null space and cannot pick up spurious information. That is the attitude-plus-bias analogue of the invariant EKF. Barrau and Bonnabel show the invariant filter converging in simulations where a standard EKF with identical tuning diverged ([Barrau & Bonnabel](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1410.1465)). Hartley et al. show that adding IMU biases breaks exact group-affinity, yet the resulting "imperfect InEKF" still **converges considerably faster than a quaternion EKF** ([Hartley et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1904.09251)). On the smoothing side, the invariant RTS smoother beat multiplicative RTS and Gauss-Newton variants, an advantage the authors attribute to its less estimate-dependent Jacobians ([van der Laan et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.00075)). PathGuard already sits on the right side of all three comparisons. One cheap refinement remains: apply the exact global reset Jacobian G = I + ½[δθ̂]× after large gravity corrections, which Solà notes "might be of interest for reducing long-term error drift" ([Solà 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.02508)).

The translation layer is also well founded. A white-noise-on-acceleration or white-noise-on-jerk trajectory prior is a linear SDE whose inverse kernel is **exactly block-tridiagonal**, so GP regression at the knot times equals ordinary Kalman/RTS smoothing ([Anderson, Barfoot et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.0630)). Estimating at keytimes and interpolating, as PathGuard's 10 Hz knots do, is the authors' own recommended cost reduction. Modelling accelerometer bias in the world frame is the right call for an unaided IMU. At rest, horizontal bias and tilt are indistinguishable ("a bias and a table that is not completely flat", in Kok, Hol and Schön's words), so a world-frame effective bias estimates exactly the combination that drives position drift ([Kok, Hol & Schön](https://arxiv.org/abs/1704.06053)). The team's rejected body-frame bias experiment (−0.5%) is consistent with this. Attitude is the dominant error path: in Woodman's 500-run study, a stationary MEMS IMU drifted 152.67 m in 60 s, gyro-induced tilt took over from accelerometer noise after about 0.3 s, and Woodman calls gyro error propagation "the critical error path in nearly all INS systems" ([Woodman 2007](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/techreports/UCAM-CL-TR-696.pdf)). PathGuard's emphasis on gravity and zero-rate updates targets that path directly.

The drag measurement f_perp = −mu·v_perp is the model of Abeywardena et al., v̇ ≈ −(k1/m)·v. In that model k1 is treated as constant only because the rotor-speed sum is roughly constant in smooth flight. The authors found the resulting linear filter robust to ±100% changes in initial values and noise variances ([Abeywardena et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1509.03388)). Martin and Salaün's point that rotor drag terms are "rather small, but... nevertheless dominant in the accelerometer measurements" is why a multirotor accelerometer observes velocity at all ([Martin & Sarras](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/1510.03249)). PathGuard's mu ≈ 0.18 1/s for the larger Firefly is plausible next to Faessler's 0.24–0.54 1/s on a 610 g quadrotor ([Faessler et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.02402)). Svacha's IMU-plus-motor-speed filter reported per-axis velocity RMS around 0.4–0.6 ([Svacha et al. 2018](https://people.computing.clemson.edu/~jmarty/projects/lowLatencyNetworking/papers/EmergingApplicationSystems/Drones-UAVs/Controllers/IntertialVelocityAndAltitudeEstimationForQuadrotors.pdf)), with units not confirmed in the snippet. That is comparable to PathGuard's raw drag residual, without motor data. **No paper reports a drag-aided, IMU-only EuRoC ATE**, so PathGuard's 15–25% gain from drag has no published counterpart ([AirIO](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15659v1) is the nearest EuRoC IMU-centric work and uses no drag model).

Two structural soft spots remain. First, motion-prior "pseudo-measurements" are statistically correct for any knot spacing or gap only if they equal the exact discrete process noise of the underlying SDE. For white-noise acceleration that is Q = q_c·[[Δt³/3, Δt²/2],[Δt²/2, Δt]] ([Anderson et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1412.0630)). Second, the per-axis translation filter cannot represent the fact that rotor-plane velocity contains a world-vertical component whenever the vehicle is tilted. Abeywardena's 2016 filter uses the raw specific force with a 3D velocity state and in-plane drag D̄ = k1(I − e3e3ᵀ) ([Abeywardena et al. 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.01486)). With attitude fixed from the smoother, that measurement stays linear in v: H = −D·Π·Rᵀ.

## "Scale" has three meanings, and only amplitude costs metres

The organizers' scoring uses evo with SE(3) alignment. evo's `-a` runs Umeyama on positions only with the scale forced to 1 ([evo geometry.py](https://github.com/MichaelGrupp/evo/blob/master/evo/core/geometry.py)). Zhang and Scaramuzza's toolbox reserves Sim(3) for monocular vision and uses 4-DoF for visual-inertial ([rpg_trajectory_evaluation](https://github.com/uzh-rpg/rpg_trajectory_evaluation)). SE(3) can therefore hide a constant tilt but **cannot forgive any amplitude error**. The Umeyama identities make this exact: ATE²_SE3 − ATE²_Sim3 = σ_x²(1 − c)², where σ_x² is the estimate's centred extent and c the Sim(3) scale. Scaling the centred estimate by k gives SE(3)-ATE²(k) = k²σ_x² + σ_y² − 2kτ, minimised at k = c ([Umeyama 1991](https://doi.org/10.1109/34.88573), as implemented in [evo](https://github.com/MichaelGrupp/evo/blob/master/evo/core/geometry.py)). RPE translation is invariant to the rigid alignment but grows by roughly (1 − k)‖Δp‖ under shrinkage ([evo metrics.py](https://github.com/MichaelGrupp/evo/blob/master/evo/core/metrics.py)). The same amplitude error therefore costs on both metrics.

**Sensor scale-factor drift is handled correctly by ignoring it as a state.** The script's random walk of 0.0015/√s reaches about 1% at 44 s. Woodman shows scale-factor errors produce position drift proportional to "the squared rate and duration of acceleration" ([Woodman 2007](https://www.cl.cam.ac.uk/techreports/UCAM-CL-TR-696.pdf)), so a 1% error on motion terms is a 1% amplitude error. The dangerous component is scale × gravity, which looks exactly like a vertical bias and is absorbed by PathGuard's world-frame bias state. Calibrating scale needs many distinct static orientations: Tedaldi uses 37–50 poses and Webering reports 12 suffice ([Webering et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2207.04801)). A flight log does not provide them. PathGuard's oracle test (1.780 vs 1.786 m) confirms the literature: under 1%. Unit auto-detection covers the third, trivial meaning.

**Trajectory amplitude is the real problem.** PathGuard's Sim(3) scales of 1.64–2.13 on machine-hall flights, 0.73–0.92 on Vicon rooms and 1.05–1.44 on TUM-VI mean the prior is mis-sized in opposite directions per environment. Shrinkage on its own is not the bug. For a calibrated Gaussian posterior mean, the orthogonality principle gives an expected regression slope of truth on estimate of 1, so E[c] ≈ 1. An optimal shrinkage estimator looks small but is not systematically too small. c ≈ 2 on one family and c ≈ 0.8 on another says the single tuned prior is too tight for the machine hall and too loose for the Vicon room. A global inflation factor cannot fix that. The team's oracle (6-flight EuRoC mean 2.43 → 1.99 m) bounds the prize at about 18%.

The literature suggests the team has not yet tested the most likely confound. Velocity from drag is −f_perp/mu, so **any error in mu scales the velocity, and hence the trajectory amplitude, one-for-one**. Faessler found mass-normalised drag coefficients varying **20–40% between trajectories on the same vehicle**, anisotropic (d_x ≠ d_y), with residuals correlated with collective thrust. The authors acknowledge their thrust-independence assumption is false ([Faessler et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.02402)). Abeywardena's coefficient is physically λ1·Σω, proportional to rotor speed ([Abeywardena et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1509.03388)). HDVIO warns that miscalibrated model coefficients get absorbed into accelerometer bias ([HDVIO](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.11429v2)). Machine-hall flights are larger and faster than Vicon-room flights, so a single mu fitted across both, plus an unmodelled quadratic term (Faessler's k_h·v_h²), would under-read velocity in one environment and over-read it in the other. That is the pattern PathGuard sees. Mu and amplitude are then confounded in the way scale and depth are in monocular VO. Amplitude may still be identifiable through a thrust proxy, but not through mu alone.

The monotonically rising marginal likelihood has a cleaner explanation than unidentifiability. If drag errors are serially correlated (PathGuard measures a correlated 0.1–0.25 m/s residual on top of white noise) but the filter treats them as white at 10 Hz, a looser prior lets the trajectory chase the correlated error and is rewarded for it. Piché's EM for a single noise intensity converges in three iterations on correctly specified models ([Piché](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1610.04397)). Misspecified noise breaks that guarantee. The fix is to make the drag noise honest before re-running the likelihood test. Either decimate drag updates to about 1 Hz on averaged specific force, or inflate R by a correlation factor (about 1 + 2τ/Δt), or carry the error as a Schmidt "consider" state. A consider state accounts for the error in the covariance without estimating it, so it avoids the failure mode of the rejected Gauss-Markov drag state, which "explains away" slow motion. If the likelihood then peaks at a finite prior size that tracks the oracle, amplitude is identifiable after all. If it still rises monotonically, the team's conclusion stands with much stronger evidence.

## Ranked gaps: drag fidelity first, then robustness plumbing

The ranking below weighs expected ATE gain against implementation effort under the pure-Python budget. It draws on the literature cited in each row and on PathGuard's own measured headroom. The gains are judgements, not measurements.

| Rank | Missing piece | Why (evidence) | Implementation hint |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Thrust-scaled, anisotropic drag with quadratic term | Coefficients vary 20–40% by trajectory and residual tracks thrust ([Faessler](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1712.02402)); coefficient ∝ rotor speed ([Abeywardena](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1509.03388)); thrust- and rotor-speed-dependent drag gives higher accuracy ([Xu & de Croon](https://www.imavs.org/papers/2023/11.pdf)) | Regress f_perp on [v_perp, (f_T/g)·v_perp, \|v\|·v_perp] per body axis on TRAIN airborne segments, where f_T is the thrust-axis specific force. First test: fit mu per flight against GT and check whether it correlates with the Sim(3) c. If it does, this is the amplitude fix. |
| 2 | Honest drag noise, then re-test prior sizing by likelihood | Correlated errors treated as white bias the likelihood toward loose priors; EM needs a correct noise model ([Piché](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1610.04397)) | Decimate to 1 Hz or set R_eff = R·(1 + 2τ/Δt); repeat the prior-size likelihood sweep per flight; accept only if the argmax tracks the oracle on TRAIN. |
| 3 | Estimated thrust axis and 2×2 drag matrix in a coupled xy(z) velocity measurement | In-plane drag operator D̄ = k1(I − e3e3ᵀ) with 3D velocity state ([Abeywardena 2016](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/1607.01486)) | Gauss-Newton over (axis n, D, offset b) on TRAIN GT. Replace per-axis horizontal updates with H = −D·Π_n·Rᵀ on a 2D or 3D [p, v] block. That is still about 4–6 states. |
| 4 | Student-t / IRLS reweighting for spikes the detector misses | Gating "often diverged" when manoeuvres and outliers both make large residuals; t-filters recommended with about 3 degrees of freedom ([Roth et al.](https://ar5iv.arxiv.org/html/1703.02428)) | Keep the hard reject at precision 0.97. For the remainder, weight w = (ν + 6)/(ν + d²) on the joint 6-channel Mahalanobis residual, re-smooth 2–3 times, and treat rejected rows as missing, not interpolated. |
| 5 | s²-consistent noise scaling and 2–3 EM iterations on (q, r) | Sage-Husa-style online adapters are reported unstable ([J. Appl. Geodesy](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/jag-2022-0048/html)); EM with an RTS E-step yields ML parameters ([Shumway & Stoffer](https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:bla:jtsera:v:3:y:1982:i:4:p:253-264)) | Check that bias-RW and white-noise variances scale with s(t)², not s(t). Fit only global multipliers r, q with Piché's M-step, using the script's known constants as MAP priors. |
| 6 | Noise-adaptive, soft rest detection | Switching thresholds by motion regime cut position RMSE by more than 50%; variance detectors are preferred for zero-rate updates ([Wahlström & Skog](https://arxiv.org/html/2008.09208v1)) | Threshold = k × (5th-percentile windowed gyro std over the log) using median/MAD statistics; R_zupt = R0/p_rest with p_rest logistic in the SHOE statistic; require a minimum fraction of real (non-dropped) rows per window. |
| 7 | Blind-window forecast tuned by masked backtests | The MMSE forecast under an OU velocity model decays velocity, x0 + v0·τ(1 − e^(−t/τ)); constant velocity is a strong pedestrian baseline at a comparable 4.8 s horizon ([Schöller et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1903.07933)) | Mask random 5 s windows on TRAIN, grid τ per platform (drones: compare hold with small τ), and revert roll/pitch to level with τ ≈ 1 s rather than only decaying the rate. |
| 8 | Constant 12-parameter gyro calibration per dataset | A 3×3 matrix plus bias took EuRoC full-sequence attitude error from 125° to 5.51° ([Brossard et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2002.10718)) | Fit on clean TRAIN logs and hard-code it. It removes intrinsic misalignment only; the injected drift still needs the online bias state. |
| 9 | Handheld cadence speed and lateral-velocity constraint (TUM-VI) | Handheld step detection about 99% and step-length error about 2–2.5% ([EOGE study](https://eoge.ut.ac.ir/article_95487_d22726ec0c01d9ec0098e541c4450486.pdf)); NHC pseudo-measurements power AI-IMU's 1.10% on KITTI ([Brossard et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06064)) | Autocorrelation cadence from 3 Hz low-passed accel norm, a Weinberg step length fitted on TRAIN, and a soft v_forward ≈ speed update. Validate first: a handheld rig damps the step signal. |

Items 1–3 attack the 18% amplitude prize, the one quantified opportunity. Items 4–6 are low-risk robustness work whose value grows late in each log, where severity peaks. Item 4 matches the script's fault structure particularly well: spikes hit all six channels in the same sample, so a single per-row weight is the correct latent model, and per-channel Huber would waste that structure. Two pieces of evaluation plumbing are worth confirming while doing this work. First, output timestamps should be strictly increasing copies of the input rows, because evo matches each pose of the shorter trajectory to its nearest neighbour within 0.01 s, many-to-one, and silently drops unmatched poses ([evo sync.py](https://github.com/MichaelGrupp/evo/blob/master/evo/core/sync.py)). Second, as a consequence, the interiors of dropout gaps are mostly unscored while the blind window is fully scored.

## Learned odometry has not beaten standing still on EuRoC

The strongest published learned IMU-only result on held-out EuRoC is AirIO with an EKF: **3.177 m average ATE**, including MH02 2.478, MH04 2.308, V1_03 3.05 and V1_01 3.844 m. RoNIN and TLIO retrained on EuRoC average **6.75 and 6.97 m**, and AirIO's tables mark both as using ground-truth orientation ([AirIO](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15659)). On those four shared flights, PathGuard scores about 3.8, 3.7, 1.20 and 1.6 m: a mean of about 2.6 m against AirIO's 2.9 m. PathGuard achieves this on degraded data, where AirIO used clean data. The comparison is not apples to apples. Alignment protocols may differ, AirIO's test set also includes V2_02, and PathGuard's numbers come from its own TRAIN/TEST split. The pattern is still informative. AirIO wins clearly on the machine hall, exactly where PathGuard shrinks by 2x, and loses clearly in the Vicon rooms. AirIO's own ablation shows body-frame features carrying gravity drive its gains, and removing gravity raised EuRoC ATE by 90% ([AirIO](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15659)). That is consistent with a learned model capturing the speed–drag relationship PathGuard approximates with a single mu. It reinforces the case for ranks 1–3.

None of these papers reports a stand-still baseline. PathGuard's own measurements show why that matters: stand-still scores 1.58 m on V1_03 and 2.30 m on V2_01, better than AirIO's V1_03 result. Learned IO's headline gains come mostly from Blackbird, where trajectories repeat and the "Seen" split trains and tests within the same trajectory. On unseen shapes, IMO degrades from 0.93 to 9.0 m and RoNIN from 2.2 to 13.5 m ([AirIO](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.15659)). The best Blackbird results also lean on thrust inputs that EuRoC lacks ([IMO](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2210.15287)). Naive delta-position regression on EuRoC drifted 9.6–17.2 m ([Tokluoglu & Keresteci](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.25454)). No learned IMU-only ATE on TUM-VI rooms was found at all. Offline Kalibr-style calibration captures almost all of AirIMU's 1 s gain there: RPE 0.1817 vs 0.1801 m ([AirIMU](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.04874)). PathGuard's 0.532 m TUM mean, against a stand-still of about 1.0 m, has no learned competitor in print.

Compute and robustness tighten the picture further. Brossard's gyro denoiser has **77,052 parameters** applied per 200 Hz sample ([Brossard et al.](https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2002.10718)). The smallest EuRoC model found, TinyOdom, needs 0.073–3.18 MFLOPs per inference at 40 inferences/s and predicts only 2D velocity ([TinyOdom](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10957141/)). A rough estimate, not from a source: CPython executes on the order of 10–50 M simple float operations per second, which puts these models 3–75x over a 20 ms/s pure-Python budget. No retrieved paper tests learned IO against spikes, frozen samples or dropped rows. Brossard augments only with mild Gaussian noise, and AirIMU uses no augmentation ([AirIMU](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.04874)). A competing team would need a network small enough for stdlib Python, trained on logs passed through the official script, that generalises to unseen flight shapes. The literature contains no such model. The realistic learned threat is the kind PathGuard can adopt itself: ridge regression on a few dozen windowed features, run at 1–5 Hz, to predict speed or to learn R(t), the stdlib analogue of AI-IMU's learned covariances ([Brossard et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06064)). The same reasoning applies to teams with stronger AI coding assistants. The binding limits are information-theoretic: yaw and absolute position are unobservable, the blind window holds no data, and amplitude depends on mu. A better assistant iterates faster against those limits but does not remove them. A team that tunes on its test flights will also look better on paper than PathGuard's flight-split protocol allows.

## Conclusion

The main shift in understanding is that PathGuard's "scale" question is a drag-model question. With a camera, scale is a property of the sensor. In a drag-aided IMU-only system it is a property of the coefficient that converts specific force into velocity. The literature's repeated finding, that this coefficient depends on thrust, axis and trajectory, gives a testable explanation for opposite-signed amplitude errors in the two EuRoC environments. That test is cheap: compare the per-flight GT-fitted mu with the per-flight Sim(3) c. It should run before amplitude is declared unidentifiable. The monotone likelihood is weaker evidence than it looks until the drag noise model is honest about correlation.

Competitively, PathGuard is already at or ahead of published learned IMU-only odometry on comparable EuRoC flights, on harsher data and a far smaller budget, and it has no published rival on TUM-VI rooms. Its exposure is concentrated in the machine-hall flights, where a learned body-frame model does measurably better. Closing that gap with physics (a better drag model) rather than a network keeps PathGuard inside the budget and keeps it robust to the degradations that learned models have never been tested against.
